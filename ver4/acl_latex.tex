\begin{abstract}
  Large-scale recommendation requires models that capture evolving user preferences while maintaining high throughput. State Space Models (SSMs) provide an efficient foundation for encoding item semantics. However, we observe that item-level ranking signals often concentrate on the most recent interactions, a phenomenon we term \emph{Nearsightedness}. To address this, we propose RIBS (\emph{R}ecommendation via \emph{I}ntegrated \emph{B}ehavioral \emph{S}ignals), which uses cached features from a frozen Mamba text encoder and separate low-rank residual modules for recommendation roles. The short-term sequence encoder reads the most recent window to model item-level behavior, while the preference transition encoder reads the full observed history to map interactions to learned prototypes and predict preference transitions. A coordinator combines their scores using transition evidence and entropy-based certainty. This design separates direct recent-item evidence from a coarser full-history preference representation without repeated language-model inference during ranking. Across diverse recommendation scenarios, RIBS consistently outperforms baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of distinct behavioral representations and coordination.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Sequential recommendation must infer what a user wants next from a history that mixes recurring interests, short-lived goals, and changing tastes. Additional interactions offer potential evidence, but their item-level details can also obscure current intent. At scale, item semantics and collaborative signals must be available to the ranker without repeatedly running an expensive language model. Mamba offers efficient sequence processing \citep{Mamba}; here, we use a frozen Mamba encoder to compute item-text features once and cache them for ranking.

We study a tendency for item-level recommendation signals to concentrate near the end of an interaction sequence, which we call \emph{nearsightedness}. Preliminary diagnostics show that the most recent interactions carry much of the measured item-level signal, while more distant items can still share a text-derived preference-proxy cluster with the next item. For example, a user may interact with several running products and then browse camping equipment: the latest items can indicate immediate intent, while recurring product themes provide a coarser view of preference. This motivates modeling recent items directly and representing preference patterns separately, without assuming that extending the item-level input will make older evidence useful.

We propose RIBS (\emph{R}ecommendation via \emph{I}ntegrated \emph{B}ehavioral \emph{S}ignals), which assigns distinct roles to a short-term sequence encoder, a preference transition encoder, and a coordinator. The short-term sequence encoder reads only the latest $W$ interactions and models their item-level order. The preference transition encoder reads the full observed history, maps its items to learned prototypes, and predicts the next preference distribution and transition probability. The coordinator combines their candidate scores according to the predicted transition and the entropy-based certainty of the next-preference distribution. Thus, older interactions enter the ranking through a coarser preference representation rather than the short-term item-level state.

The central design choice is to use different history horizons for complementary evidence: a short recent window for item-level sequential modeling and the full observed history for prototype-level preference modeling. Cached Mamba \cite{Mamba} text features and LightGCN \cite{LightGCN} embeddings give both encoders a shared item space, while separate low-rank residual maps adapt their representations. The coordinator then varies the preference contribution according to the predicted transition context.

We evaluate the contributions of recent behavior and preference representations on four Amazon Reviews 2023 categories using full-catalog ranking. Combining the short-term and preference branches attains the highest displayed NDCG and Recall scores in all four categories. Our contributions are the nearsightedness-motivated formulation, an architecture coordinating a short-term sequence encoder with a preference transition encoder over shared item semantics, and an analysis of how ranking varies with the available behavioral context.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Sequential representation learning.}
Sequential recommenders first model user behavior as ordered item interactions. SASRec~\citep{SASRec} uses self-attention to select relevant past items, BERT4Rec~\citep{BERT4Rec} learns bidirectional sequence representations with masked-item prediction, and TiSASRec~\citep{TiSASRec} incorporates time intervals. These models establish sequence context as a source of next-item evidence. Text-aware work adds semantic information to this setting: Recformer~\citep{Recformer} represents item attributes as language, EasyRec~\citep{EasyRec} aligns textual and collaborative representations, and BLaIR~\citep{BLaIR} learns recommendation-oriented item representations from text. This line of work motivates our shared item space, in which cached text features complement collaborative embeddings. Related text-based recommendation work also includes UniTRec~\citep{UnitRec}, and MAPLE~\citep{MAPLE}, which study text-to-text recommendation, and explanation generation, respectively. LLM-based approaches extend semantic modeling to recommendation: ELMRec~\citep{ELMRec} models higher-order interactions, while D$^3$~\citep{D3} studies decoding bias and recommendation diversity. Our focus is on ranking items from interaction histories using precomputed language representations.

\paragraph{Selective state-space models.}
Mamba~\citep{Mamba} uses input-dependent state updates and hardware-aware scans to process long sequences efficiently. This efficiency is relevant to recommendation, where user histories must be encoded at scale. Mamba4Rec~\citep{Mamba4Rec} and SIGMA~\citep{SIGMA} apply selective state-space modeling to interaction sequences, while T2ARec~\citep{T2ARec} uses an SSM to adapt to shifts in user interests. These methods motivate efficient behavioral modeling. In our setting, a pretrained Mamba encoder instead produces frozen item-text features once; the ranker combines those cached features with separate recent-behavior and preference-transition representations.

\paragraph{Graph-augmented recommendation.}
Interaction graphs provide collaborative evidence that item text alone cannot capture. LightGCN \citep{LightGCN} propagates embeddings over the user-item interaction graph; we use its item embeddings alongside cached text features in the shared item space. RLMRec \citep{RLMRec} aligns LLM-derived semantic profiles with collaborative representations, and LLMRec \citep{LLMRec} augments user-item graphs with LLM-generated information. M2GRec \citep{M2GRec} combines Mamba sequence modeling with item-transition and user-item graphs, connecting efficient sequence modeling with collaborative structure. Other graph-augmented recommendation work uses different relations: CoLaKG \citep{CoLaKG} and K-RagRec \citep{K-RagRec} incorporate external knowledge graphs, while GeoMamba \citep{GeoMamba} and STMamba-GC \citep{STMamba-GC} model geographical and spatiotemporal relations for POI recommendation. These studies provide context for graph-enhanced recommendation.


\paragraph{Low-rank adaptation.}
LoRA \citep{LoRA} parameterizes weight updates with two low-rank matrices while keeping the pretrained backbone frozen, reducing fine-tuning memory without modifying the original model weights. This property enables multiple LoRA adapters to share a single backbone, supporting efficient multi-task and multi-agent applications \citep{MapCoder-Lite}.

\subsection{Problem Definition}
Let $\mathcal I$ be an item catalog and $\mathcal H_{u,t}=(i_1,\ldots,i_t)$ the interactions observed for user $u$ before the next-item prediction. The short-term sequence encoder receives the bounded suffix $\mathcal R_{u,t}^{(W)}=(i_{\max(1,t-W+1)},\ldots,i_t)$, while the preference transition encoder receives $\mathcal H_{u,t}$ in full. RIBS assigns each candidate $i\in\mathcal I$ a score $z(u,i\mid\mathcal H_{u,t})$ by combining item-level evidence from the suffix with prototype-level evidence from the full history.

\subsection{Preliminary Analysis}
\label{sec:preliminary}

Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary} combines three text-based diagnostics, panels (a), (c), and (d), with a history-deletion diagnostic, panel (b), from a trained full-history ranker using Mamba item-text features and LightGCN collaborative representations. Lag 1 is the last observed item before the target. In panel (a), the vertical axis is the historical item's text cosine similarity to the next item minus the mean similarity of popularity-matched control items, divided by a category-wide cosine standard deviation. Thus, positive values indicate greater text similarity than the matched controls. In panel (b), the vertical axis is the change in the next item's score minus the mean score of fixed sampled negatives when one historical item is deleted; positive values mean that retaining that item increases this ranking margin. In panel (c), the vertical axis is the percentage of each user's positive excess text similarity from panel (a) contributed by the most recent $k$ positions, averaged over users; the denominator includes up to 100 observed positions. This percentage describes text association, not the trained model's attribution. Appendix~\ref{app:preliminary_diagnostics} gives the exact estimators, sampling, and uncertainty intervals.

The next item has its strongest text-based semantic association with the most recent interactions, and this association falls quickly as lag grows (Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary}a). The deletion-based change in the sampled-slate margin is likewise concentrated in roughly the latest four positions; older positions are near zero and can be negative in the plotted categories (Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary}b). The cumulative positive text-similarity excess reaches approximately 80\% within the latest four items (Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary}c). These descriptive observations motivate separating direct item-level evidence from coarser preference evidence.

The weak item-level signal at longer lags raises a more specific question: can older interactions remain informative when represented by a recurring interest rather than an exact item? For Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary}d, we fit 32 MiniBatchKMeans groups to hashed item-text features from sampled training items and assign each usable item a hard group label $c(i)$. 
% This is a model-free preference proxy: it is independent of the RIBS preference transition encoder, uses neither next-item ranking supervision nor the Mamba and LightGCN item vectors, and differs from the learned soft prototype assignments in RIBS. Two different items can nevertheless share a proxy group.

To test whether this coarser representation retains information at longer lags, let $y_u$ be user $u$'s next item, and let $p_{u,B}$ be the fraction of that user's usable, control-matched historical positions in lag bin $B$ for which $c(i)=c(y_u)$. Let $\mathcal C_u$ be the distinct proxy-group labels in the user's analyzed history. We compare the match fraction with $b_u=\mathbf{1}[c(y_u)\in\mathcal C_u]/|\mathcal C_u|$, the probability of a match when drawing uniformly from those labels. Panel (d)'s vertical axis is the excess match probability, in percentage points:
\begin{equation}
 E_B=\frac{100}{|\mathcal U_B|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal U_B}
 \left[p_{u,B}-b_u\right],
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal U_B$ contains users with at least one usable, control-matched position in bin $B$. A positive value means that past items in that bin share the next item's proxy group more often than this user-specific uniform-label reference. Appendix~\ref{app:preliminary_match} specifies the within-user averaging, group fitting, and chance reference.

Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary}d shows that this excess proxy-cluster match does not always fade with distance: it rises again at longer lags in most categories. This suggests that coarse text similarity can remain visible where direct item-level evidence is weak; it does not establish that the trained RIBS preference encoder uses those older interactions. The inverse-history control in Appendix~\ref{app:preliminary_inverse} retrains the Mamba+LightGCN ranker on reversed histories and repeats the text diagnostics in that order. It helps distinguish the relevance of originally recent items from a tendency to concentrate model influence near the end of the input. Together, these diagnostics motivate reading the full history through a coarser preference representation while limiting direct item-level modeling to the recent window.

\subsection{RIBS}
\label{sec:ribs}

\subsubsection{Task and Shared Item Space}
Let $\mathcal I$ be the item catalog and $\mathcal H_{u,t}=(i_1,\ldots,i_t)$ a user's observed prefix. The task is to score each candidate next item $i\in\mathcal I$. The short-term sequence encoder reads the suffix of length $T_S=\min(t,W)$, with input vectors $x_k=e_{i_{t-T_S+k}}$ for $k=1,\ldots,T_S$. The preference transition encoder instead reads all $t$ observed items through their prototype assignments. Training uses subsequent logged interactions as targets; validation and test each contain one target per eligible user. The short-term window limits only direct item-level sequence modeling, while collaborative representations use the training interaction graph.

For each item, we encode its text once with a frozen Mamba model. The input begins with ``Preference-aware product representation: ''. We mean-pool content-token hidden states, excluding padding and prefix tokens; if truncation leaves no content tokens, the implementation falls back to pooling non-padding tokens. Only backbone hidden states are computed; vocabulary prediction logits are unnecessary. The resulting vector $f_i$ is cached and reused during ranker training and evaluation. A trainable projection maps it to the recommendation dimension $d$.
The shared item vector $e_i$ combines the projected text feature with a standard collaborative item embedding from LightGCN \citep{LightGCN}. This graph component is common to all reported variants; its construction and normalization are specified in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}.

Each encoder and the coordinator have distinct low-rank residual maps. For module $j$, the LoRA update is
\begin{equation}
 y_j=\mathcal R_j(x)=\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}}{r}B_j A_j\operatorname{Dropout}(x),
\end{equation}
Here $y_j$ is the residual output of module $j$. Matrix $A_j\in\mathbb R^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in},j}}$ projects to rank $r$, and $B_j\in\mathbb R^{d_{\mathrm{out},j}\times r}$ projects to the output dimension. Dropout is inactive at inference, and each $B_j$ is zero-initialized. 
% The eight module labels are $j\in\{\mathrm{step},\mathrm{cand},\mathrm{gate},\mathrm{out},\mathrm{pref},\mathrm{recon},\mathrm{coord},\mathrm{score}\}$. These maps adapt the recommendation modules while the pretrained text encoder remains frozen.

\subsubsection{Short-Term Sequence Encoder}
The short-term sequence encoder models item-level evidence in the bounded interaction window. For each item vector $x_k$, $1\leq k\leq T_S$, it updates its state as
\begin{align}
 a_k &= \exp(-\min\{\operatorname{softplus}(b_\delta
              +\mathcal R_{\mathrm{step}}(x_k)),12\}),\notag \\
 h_k &= a_k\odot h_{k-1}\notag\\
     &\quad +(1-a_k)\odot\tanh(x_k+\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cand}}(x_k)),\notag \\
 o_k &= \sigma(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{gate}}(x_k))\odot h_k\notag\\
     &\quad +(1-\sigma(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{gate}}(x_k)))\odot x_k.
\end{align}
The initial state is zero, and padding leaves the state and output unchanged. The factor $a_k$ controls how much of the previous state is retained: smaller values give more weight to the current item. The final short-term representation is $s_S=\norm{o_{T_S}+\mathcal R_{\mathrm{out}}(o_{T_S})}$. Its four low-rank modules parameterize the retention factor, candidate state, output gate, and final residual. The bias $b_\delta$ is initialized so that $a_k=\exp(-0.5)$ when the zero-initialized residual map has no effect; the retention factor then adapts during training.

The recurrence is linear in window length at fixed model dimension; full-catalog scoring additionally scales with catalog size.

\subsubsection{Preference Transition Encoder}
The preference transition encoder tracks transitions among recurring preference patterns instead of retaining each past item as a separate signal.
Let $C\in\mathbb R^{M\times d}$ contain row-normalized learned prototypes. Each interaction in the full observed history is represented by a soft assignment
\begin{equation}
 q_t=\softmax(Ce_{i_t}/\tau_p).
\end{equation}
A GRU reads $(q_1,\ldots,q_t)$. Its final hidden state $\xi_t=\operatorname{GRU}(q_1,\ldots,q_t)$ yields $v_t=\operatorname{LN}(\xi_t+\mathcal R_{\mathrm{pref}}(\xi_t))$ after a low-rank residual and layer normalization. The current preference is the last observed assignment $q_t$. Two heads predict the next distribution and transition probability:
\begin{align}
 \widehat q_{t+1}&=\softmax(W_n v_t+b_n),\notag\\
 \rho_t&=\sigma(w_c^\top[v_t;q_t]+b_c).
\end{align}
The distribution yields a preference vector,
\begin{equation}
 s_P=\norm{C^\top\widehat q_{t+1}+\mathcal R_{\mathrm{recon}}(C^\top\widehat q_{t+1})},
\end{equation}
% which scores candidates through a learned scaled inner product. Prototypes are shared among users and items within a run; separate categories train separate parameters. Prototype indices have no predefined semantic labels and need not align across runs.
which scores candidates by a scaled inner product. Prototypes are shared across users and items within each run; categories use separate parameters. Prototype indices have no predefined semantics or cross-run alignment.

For either encoder $m\in\{S,P\}$, candidate scores use the shared item vectors:
\begin{equation}
 z_m(u,i)=\kappa_m s_m^\top e_i,\;
 \kappa_m=\min\{\exp(\theta_m),20\}.
\end{equation}
The shared text projection, graph embeddings, and preference predictor are trainable during encoder and joint optimization.

\subsubsection{Context-Dependent Coordination}
The coordinator constructs a residual refinement of the short-term sequence encoder state:
\begin{align}
 s_C&=\norm{s_S+\mathcal R_{\mathrm{coord}}(s_S)},\notag\\
 \widetilde s_C&=\norm{s_C+\mathcal R_{\mathrm{score}}(s_C)},\notag\\
 z_C(u,i)&=\kappa_C\widetilde s_C^\top e_i.
\end{align}
Here $\mathcal R_{\mathrm{coord}}$ maps the short-term state to the coordinator state, and $\kappa_C=\min\{\exp(\theta_C),20\}$. This refinement adds a coordinator-specific scoring path while preserving the direct encoder scores.

The preference contribution uses transition probability and entropy-based certainty, with $H(\cdot)$ denoting Shannon entropy:
\begin{align}
 c_t&=1-\frac{H(\widehat q_{t+1})}{\log M},\notag\\
 \gamma_u&=\sigma(b_\gamma+w_\gamma^\top[\rho_t;c_t]),\notag\\
 z(u,i)&=z_C(u,i)+z_S(u,i)+\gamma_u z_P(u,i).
\end{align}
The scalar $b_\gamma$ includes the learned gate intercept, initialized so that $\gamma_u=0.2$; the context weights initially vanish. The gate subsequently learns how transition evidence and entropy-based certainty should affect preference scores. The certainty $c_t$ measures concentration of the predicted distribution, not calibrated prediction accuracy. The fused score adds the two encoder scores and the coordinator score, with a learned weight on the preference score. The coordinator's state refinement uses $s_S$, while preference predictions influence the final ranking through $z_P$ and $\gamma_u$.

Eight low-rank residual modules participate in this computation: four in the short-term sequence encoder, two in the preference transition encoder, and two in the coordinator. Their parameters are distinct, while all three components share the same item representations.

\paragraph{Catalog calibration at inference.}
At evaluation, training-only statistics adjust neural scores:
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde z(u,i)=z(u,i)+\alpha_p p_i+\beta_t\log(1+n_{i_t,i}),
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ is standardized log-one-plus training popularity and $n_{i_t,i}$ counts adjacent training transitions from the last observed item. Missing transitions contribute zero. These coefficients are configured by category. All reported results include these priors; the current experiments do not isolate their contribution from that of the neural model.

\subsubsection{Training Objective}
\paragraph{Multi-step ranking.}
For each sampled prefix, we collect up to $F$ future items from the training history only. Their weights are $a_j=\eta^{j-1}/\sum_{k=1}^{F'}\eta^{k-1}$, where $F'\leq F$ is the available horizon. With candidate slate $\mathcal S$ and target position $p_j$, a ranking head minimizes
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}(z)
 =-\sum_{j=1}^{F'}a_j\log\softmax(z_{\mathcal S})_{p_j}.
\end{equation}
Bounded training prefixes are resampled each epoch. Candidate slates combine future targets with model-mined and random negatives drawn from an enlarged pool, augmented with in-batch future items. Known training positives are masked during negative scoring. The hard-negative fraction increases during warm-up. Candidate slates retain a fixed number of future-target slots: unavailable future positions repeat the last available target but have zero target weight and are masked from the softmax denominator. Thus, $\softmax(z_{\mathcal S})$ in the ranking loss is computed over valid slate positions only. Sampling is over item positions, so candidate identities need not be unique.

\paragraph{Preference supervision.}
The immediate next item's prototype assignment supplies a detached target $q^+=\sg(q(i_{t+1}))$. We minimize $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pred}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^+\Vert\widehat q_{t+1})$. The transition target is
\begin{equation}
 y_{\mathrm{shift}}=\frac{\operatorname{JS}(\sg(q_t),q^+)}{\log 2},
\end{equation}
and $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{shift}}$ is binary cross-entropy between this soft target and $\rho_t$. This target measures a change in learned item assignments, rather than an annotated change in user intent.

Three additional terms control prototype learning: a KL penalty between the batch mean of non-detached next-item assignments $q(i_{t+1})$ and the uniform distribution, a squared penalty on $CC^\top-I$, and normalized assignment entropy to encourage sharper individual assignments. Together,
\begin{align}
 \mathcal L_P={}&\lambda_p\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pred}}
 +\lambda_t\mathcal L_{\mathrm{shift}}
 +\lambda_b\mathcal L_{\mathrm{balance}}\nonumber\notag\\
 &+\lambda_s\mathcal L_{\mathrm{separation}}
 +\lambda_h\mathcal L_{\mathrm{sharpness}}.
\end{align}
Unlike the prediction targets, the balance, separation, and sharpness terms propagate gradients through assignments or prototypes.

\paragraph{Staged optimization.}
Let $\mathcal L_S=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}(z_S)$, $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pref}}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}(z_P)$, and $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{fused}}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}(z)$. Encoder warm-up updates both encoders and the shared item representations with
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{warm}}=\mathcal L_S+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pref}}+\mathcal L_P.
\end{equation}
Coordinator warm-up freezes these components and minimizes $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{fused}}$ with respect to the coordinator's residual maps, score scale, and preference gate. Joint training updates all these recommendation components using
\begin{align}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{joint}}={}&\frac{\mathcal L_S+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pref}}+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{fused}}}{3}
 +\mathcal L_P\nonumber\\
 &+\lambda_{\mathrm{con}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{con}}
 +\lambda_{\mathrm{div}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{div}}.
\end{align}
The diversity term averages $(s_S^\top s_P)^2$ over the batch, separating encoder states. Contrastive alignment compares preference states with next-item vectors in the batch:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{con}}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{u=1}^{N}
 \log\frac{\sum_{v:y_v=y_u}\exp(s_{P,u}^\top e_{y_v}/\tau_c)}
 {\sum_{v=1}^{N}\exp(s_{P,u}^\top e_{y_v}/\tau_c)},
\end{equation}
where $y_u$ is the immediate next item, $N$ is batch size, and $\tau_c=0.1$. The numerator includes all batch targets sharing the same item identity. All stages optimize supervised ranking on logged training interactions; the pretrained Mamba encoder is unchanged throughout.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
We evaluate RIBS on four Amazon Reviews 2023 categories using full-catalog Recall and NDCG. Our central ablations ask whether the short-term sequence encoder and preference transition encoder complement each other, and whether adding direct item-level long-term memory helps the combined model. We then examine history-length cohorts and sensitivity to the recent window, preference granularity, and auxiliary objectives. Dataset construction, evaluation details, configurations, and run provenance are given in Appendices~\ref{app:dataset_protocol}, \ref{app:implementation}, and \ref{app:runs}. All results are single-seed point estimates.

\subsection{Ablation of Behavioral Evidence}
\paragraph{Short-term behavior and preference.}
Table~\ref{tab:amazon2023_main} compares our short-term plus preference model with variants that remove either branch. The combined model has the highest displayed NDCG@10 in every category. Preference alone is less effective than recent item-level behavior, but it improves ranking when paired with that behavior. The single-branch variants also omit coordinator training, so these rows test the configurations as a whole rather than isolating one representation.

\paragraph{Adding direct long-term memory.}
Table~\ref{tab:long_addition} compares our model with the same recorded configuration augmented by a long-horizon temporal encoder. Adding that branch lowers NDCG@10 in all four categories; the effect on other metrics is less uniform. This pattern is consistent with the proposed nearsightedness motivation: direct item-level access to more history does not necessarily help next-item ranking. It is not a causal test of history noise, because the added branch also changes model capacity and optimization. The other recorded component combinations are retained in Appendix~\ref{app:all_components}.

\subsection{Performance across User-History Lengths}
We group users by their complete filtered sequence length before the validation/test holdout, independently of the model's context window. The short-history cohort dominates the aggregate metrics; the group definition and sizes are in Appendix~\ref{app:dataset_protocol}.

Table~\ref{tab:main_length_groups} shows that, on All Beauty, both the short-term plus preference model and its long-branch extension improve on the single temporal branch in both cohorts. The relative gain is larger among long-history users, but that cohort is much smaller. Since our model performs similarly without the long branch, this pattern does not demonstrate a benefit from explicitly processing a longer item-level sequence.

The single long-horizon branch also scores lower for long-history users than for short-history users in the other categories. The cohorts may differ in item distribution and prediction difficulty, so this is not evidence that giving a model more history causes worse recommendations. Full-model cohort results for those categories are unavailable, and cohort rows from different runs are not combined to reconstruct overall results.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Recent-History Window}
Table~\ref{tab:short_window} varies the recent-history window of the short-term sequence encoder in $S+P$. The preference transition encoder still reads the full observed history, and graph information still uses all training interactions.

The shortest tested window performs best across the reported metrics in Baby Products, Sports and Outdoors, and Toys and Games, including within both history-length cohorts. Window choice produces larger changes than the prototype-count and individual-loss sweeps. This supports the importance of local behavior when full-history preference modeling is retained.

All Beauty is an exception: its overall ranking changes little across windows, and its preferred window differs between history-length cohorts. The test-set sweeps characterize sensitivity rather than a validation-selected replacement for the default setting.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Preference Granularity}
Table~\ref{tab:prefsweep} varies the number of learned preference prototypes. The full-model sweep has missing settings, so comparisons use the available rows within each configuration.

More prototypes do not improve ranking monotonically. All Beauty favors an intermediate count, whereas small prototype inventories are competitive in the other categories. These differences are modest, and single-seed results cannot establish that granularity has no effect.

\subsection{Effect of Preference Auxiliary Objectives}
Table~\ref{tab:coefzero} removes each of six auxiliary terms in turn: next-preference prediction, transition detection, prototype balance, prototype separation, assignment sharpness, and contrastive alignment. The final row removes all six. These controls retain the preference branch and ranking supervision; they therefore test the auxiliary objectives rather than the presence of preference-based scoring.

Removing individual auxiliary terms has little effect on the displayed ranking metrics, and no term is consistently required for the strongest result.

Removing the entire auxiliary-loss bundle likewise yields no consistent ranking disadvantage and improves some Baby Products metrics. A preference branch can still help through ranking supervision even when these additional losses show little effect. Ranking metrics alone do not assess transition-prediction quality or prototype calibration.

\subsection{Discussion}
The experiments support combining recent temporal evidence with a preference representation: $S+P$ leads the main ablation on NDCG@10, while adding a direct long-horizon encoder lowers that metric in all four categories. Among the sensitivity experiments, context-window choice produces the largest observed changes on the three larger categories. Prototype count and auxiliary coefficients have smaller effects. This pattern suggests that the amount and source of behavioral context deserve particular attention when configuring the model. The sweeps were conducted separately, however, and do not establish the behavior of a configuration that combines their individual best settings.

The evidence supports offline ranking observations rather than a causal attribution to any one mechanism. All component rows retain graph features and catalog priors, and disabling encoders can alter both sequence horizon and optimization. Multiple-seed comparisons, matched external baselines, and controls for shared text features, graph information, priors, and coordinator behavior would be needed to resolve these contributions. The tables also contain no measurements of latency or memory, so computational savings from cached encoding remain an architectural motivation rather than a measured experimental result.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a sequential ranker that combines cached Mamba text representations, recent-behavior modeling, and preference-transition prediction. The architecture keeps language encoding outside the ranking optimization loop and uses compact low-rank modules for branch-specific transformations. The short-term plus preference configuration outperforms its single-branch variants on NDCG@10 in all four categories, while adding a direct long-horizon temporal branch lowers that metric in the recorded comparison. Window-size experiments reveal larger changes than the prototype-count and auxiliary-loss controls. These results support compact temporal--preference cooperation while showing that more item-level history and auxiliary supervision do not guarantee stronger ranking performance.

\section*{Limitations}
The evidence is limited to existing offline runs with one recorded seed per configuration. There are no confidence intervals, significance tests, or verified matched external baseline reruns. Overall full-model scores are available for all four categories, but full-model cohort scores are missing for three categories. Some overall and cohort rows correspond to different recorded runs, and the sensitivity sweeps contain missing settings. Some duplicate runs predate explicit history-mode metadata; selecting the later auditable runs improves traceability but does not establish bitwise reproducibility. The saved artifacts do not pin every run to an immutable source revision, and the suite does not persist model weights.

The ablations change more than parameter presence. Disabling the long branch changes model capacity and optimization, and single-encoder variants omit the coordinator stage. All reported variants retain graph information and category-specific ranking priors. Separate controls are needed to attribute improvements to semantic features, the preference objectives, coordination, or the priors. Although state updates scale linearly with sequence length, we have not measured end-to-end latency, peak memory, energy use, or training cost under matched hardware.

The evaluation is transductive and based on per-user chronological splits. Count filtering and catalog construction use the full observed data before splitting. Missing metadata may fall back to review content assembled before the split, potentially exposing information unavailable at recommendation time; the frequency and impact of this fallback have not been audited. Thus excluding held-out events from graph and prior construction is not a complete temporal leakage audit. The single-pass count filter also differs from iterative 5-core protocols.

Prototype transitions are self-supervised signals derived from learned item embeddings. Their identities are not human-interpretable categories, and high transition probability need not indicate a real change in personal taste. The experiments do not establish explanation faithfulness, conversational recommendation quality, cold-start generalization, or multilingual robustness. Finally, logged reviews do not describe exposure propensities or responses to alternative recommendations. Offline ranking gains do not establish causal improvements in satisfaction, retention, fairness, or user welfare.

\section{Methods for the Preliminary Diagnostics}
\label{app:preliminary_diagnostics}

\paragraph{Evaluation sample and interaction lag.}
We analyze All Beauty, Baby Products, Sports and Outdoors, and Toys and Games using the same one-pass minimum-five-interaction filter and chronological leave-two-out split as the main experiments (Appendix~\ref{app:dataset_protocol}). For each test prediction, the observed history contains the training interactions followed by the validation item; the held-out test item is the target. We retain at most the last 100 observed interactions. Lag $\ell=1$ denotes the final item in this input, and lag counts interactions rather than elapsed time. The text-based diagnostics in panels (a), (c), and (d) sample up to 2,000 users uniformly without replacement from those eligible for both holdouts, with seed 25252. All 1,457 eligible All Beauty users are included; the other three categories each contribute 2,000 users. Panel (b) instead selects up to 2,000 equally spaced users from the sorted eligible set. The two selection rules need not yield identical users, so comparisons across panel types are descriptive.

\paragraph{Text representation and matched controls.}
For panels (a), (c), and (d), item text is mapped to a 1,024-dimensional feature-hashing representation using word unigrams and bigrams, English stop-word removal, nonnegative hashing, and $\ell_2$ normalization. Let $v_i$ be the resulting unit vector for item $i$; zero vectors are excluded. These diagnostic vectors are distinct from the frozen Mamba and LightGCN features used by the trained ranker. Up to 20,000 distinct items observed in training are sampled uniformly as the reference pool. Training-item frequencies $n_i$ are divided into five popularity bins using the empirical $0.2$, $0.4$, $0.6$, and $0.8$ quantiles of $\log(1+n_i)$, with ties assigned to the higher bin. For a historical item $h_{u,\ell}$, we draw 20 training-reference controls from its popularity bin after excluding the user's observed history. An empty bin is widened successively to adjacent bins; sampling uses replacement only when fewer than 20 eligible controls remain. Controls with unusable text are discarded. The held-out target is not separately excluded from this control pool and may be sampled if it occurs among training-reference items.

A separate cosine baseline is estimated within each category from 100,000 draws of distinct item pairs from the full processed catalog, retaining pairs with nonzero text vectors. Draws may repeat across pairs. Let $\mu_D$ and $\sigma_D$ be the sample mean and sample standard deviation, respectively, with the latter using denominator $N-1$. We use $s(i,j)=v_i^\top v_j$ and $z_D(i,j)=[s(i,j)-\mu_D]/\sigma_D$. The full catalog defines this normalization, whereas controls and preference-proxy fitting use training items.

\paragraph{Panel (a): semantic alignment.}
For target $y_u$ and the usable matched-control multiset $R_{u,\ell}$, the position-level excess alignment is
\begin{equation}
 d^{\mathrm{sem}}_{u,\ell}
 =z_D(h_{u,\ell},y_u)
 -\frac{1}{|R_{u,\ell}|}\sum_{r\in R_{u,\ell}}z_D(r,y_u).
 \label{eq:prelim_semantic}
\end{equation}
The common centering term cancels, so this equals the raw excess cosine divided by $\sigma_D$. A position is excluded if the target, historical item, or every sampled control has unusable text, or if no control is eligible. We group lags into $[1,1]$, $[2,2]$, $[3,4]$, $[5,8]$, $[9,16]$, $[17,32]$, $[33,64]$, and $[65,100]$. For bin $B$, let $\mathcal J_{u,B}$ be user $u$'s usable positions and $\mathcal U_B$ the users with at least one such position. Each user contributes one within-bin mean, and the plotted estimate is
\begin{equation}
 \bar d_B=\frac{1}{|\mathcal U_B|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal U_B}
 \frac{1}{|\mathcal J_{u,B}|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal J_{u,B}}
 d^{\mathrm{sem}}_{u,\ell}.
 \label{eq:prelim_bin_mean}
\end{equation}
Shading shows the $2.5$th and $97.5$th percentiles of 2,000 bootstrap means obtained by resampling users with replacement, using seed $25252+\min(B)+\max(B)$ for each bin. Points lie at bin midpoints on a linear lag axis; the horizontal zero line denotes equality with matched controls. This statistic measures text association, not ranking accuracy.

\paragraph{Panel (b): trained-model history-item influence.}
This diagnostic uses a single full-history Mamba sequence branch with LightGCN item features; the short-term and preference branches and coordinator are absent. The pretrained Mamba-130M text encoder is frozen, while the ranker uses dimension 128 and rank-16 low-rank residuals with scale 32 and dropout 0.05. Item text is truncated to 32 tokens and histories to 100 interactions. The model is trained for three joint-ranking epochs with batch size 128 and learning rate $5\times10^{-5}$, then restored to the state with the best validation NDCG@10. The training transition caps for the four categories, in the order above, are $0.5$, $1.5$, $1.0$, and $1.5$ million; their candidate counts are 64, 192, 256, and 192. Validation is performed once per epoch on all eligible validation users. Graph construction and any count-based priors use training interactions only.

For each selected test user, we sample up to 64 distinct negatives uniformly from the catalog, excluding the target and observed items (seed 25252). The same negative set $N_u$ is used for the full history and every single-position deletion. We use the restored model's raw score $f(i\mid H)$, without the popularity or transition priors used in full-catalog evaluation, to define
\begin{align}
 m_u(H)&=f(y_u\mid H)-\frac{1}{|N_u|}\sum_{n\in N_u}f(n\mid H),\\
 \Delta_{u,\ell}&=m_u(H_u)-m_u(H_u\setminus\{h_{u,\ell}\}).
 \label{eq:prelim_influence}
\end{align}
The deletion shortens the input sequence. A positive $\Delta_{u,\ell}$ means that retaining the item increases the target's margin over these fixed negatives; it is not a change in full-catalog Recall or NDCG. At each exact lag, we plot the user mean and a 95\% percentile interval from 2,000 user-bootstrap replicates, seeded $25252+\ell$. The combined figure displays lags with at least 100 contributing users; the separate all-position display retains sparser lags. Margin scales are not calibrated across categories.

\paragraph{Panel (c): cumulative positive semantic alignment.}
Using the same position-level values as panel (a), set $a_{u,\ell}=\max(d^{\mathrm{sem}}_{u,\ell},0)$ for usable positions and zero otherwise, and let $A_u=\sum_{\ell=1}^{100}a_{u,\ell}$. Users with $A_u=0$ are excluded. The coverage at the most recent $k$ positions is
\begin{equation}
 C_u(k)=100\,\frac{\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}a_{u,\ell}}{A_u},
 \qquad 1\leq k\leq100.
 \label{eq:prelim_coverage}
\end{equation}
The curve is the unweighted mean of $C_u(k)$ across included users. Its band is the cross-user $25$th--$75$th percentile range, rather than a confidence interval. The combined figure displays $k\leq20$ and marks 80\%; the separate coverage plot displays all 100 positions and marks 50\%, 80\%, and 90\%. These percentages quantify the distribution of positive excess text similarity, not model attribution or recommendation accuracy.

\subsection{Panel (d): Preference-Proxy Match}
\label{app:preliminary_match}
We fit 32 MiniBatchKMeans groups to at most 10,000 nonzero training-reference text vectors (seed 25252, batch size at most 1,024, three initializations, at most 100 iterations). The fitted groups assign each usable item a hard label $c(i)\in\{0,\ldots,31\}$. They are text-derived proxies, independent of the RIBS preference prototypes, next-item ranking supervision, and Mamba or LightGCN features.

Let $\mathcal C_u$ be the distinct labels among usable items in the entire observed history of user $u$. Users with an unusable target or no usable history label are excluded. If one label is selected uniformly from $\mathcal C_u$, its probability of matching the target is
\begin{equation}
 b_u=\frac{\mathbf 1[c(y_u)\in\mathcal C_u]}{|\mathcal C_u|}.
 \label{eq:prelim_preference_baseline}
\end{equation}
This baseline is zero when the target label is absent and generally differs from $1/32$. At each usable, control-matched position, define $d^{\mathrm{pref}}_{u,\ell}=\mathbf 1[c(h_{u,\ell})=c(y_u)]-b_u$. Using the same within-user then across-user aggregation as panel (a), the plotted excess agreement in percentage points is
\begin{equation}
 E_B=\frac{100}{|\mathcal U_B|}
 \sum_{u\in\mathcal U_B}\frac{1}{|\mathcal J_{u,B}|}
 \sum_{\ell\in\mathcal J_{u,B}}d^{\mathrm{pref}}_{u,\ell}.
 \label{eq:prelim_preference_match}
\end{equation}
The shaded interval uses the same user-bootstrap procedure as panel (a); zero indicates agreement at the user's uniform-label chance level. The confidence intervals in panels (a), (b), and (d) condition on the selected users and fixed diagnostic representations; they do not capture variation across model-training seeds.

\paragraph{Cohort displays and plotting conventions.}
The additional two-panel alignment and preference displays compare all users with a fixed-history cohort requiring at least 16 observed interactions. Estimates for fixed minimum lengths of 8 and 32 are computed in the same way, retaining only bins whose upper edge does not exceed the minimum length. A category curve is shown only where a point has at least 100 contributing users; consequently, Baby Products does not appear in the fixed-16 panel. A separate paired contrast compares each qualifying user's lag-1 value with that user's mean over lags 9--16, then bootstraps these within-user differences.

Across the four-category plots, All Beauty is blue with a solid line and circles, Baby Products orange with a dashed line and squares, Sports and Outdoors green with a dash-dot line and triangles, and Toys and Games pink with a dotted line and diamonds. Category estimates are overlaid without pooling or smoothing. Binned estimates use the arithmetic bin midpoint on a linear x-axis; influence uses exact integer lag. Zero-reference lines appear for excess alignment, excess preference agreement, and margin influence. The combined coverage display is restricted visually to $k=1,\ldots,20$, while its denominator still includes up to 100 positions. The individual influence display includes all available lags; the individual coverage display uses the full $k=1,\ldots,100$ range. The inverse-history display retains the same estimators and visual conventions, but reverses each capped input history before calculating lags and retrains the influence model on that order. Thus, its lag 1 is the originally oldest interaction within the retained suffix; chronological targets, splits, graph, and training-item priors remain unchanged.

\section{Dataset and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{app:dataset_protocol}
We analyze completed Amazon Reviews 2023 runs on All Beauty, Baby Products, Sports and Outdoors, and Toys and Games \citep{BLaIR}. All Beauty is a distinct Amazon category from Beauty and Personal Care. Item text concatenates available titles, subtitles, categories, features, and descriptions. Missing metadata can fall back to review text or an item identifier. The Amazon loader treats review events as implicit interactions and does not apply the rating threshold used by other dataset adapters.

Preprocessing counts raw user and item interactions and applies a minimum count of five once. It is not iterative 5-core filtering: removing low-frequency items may leave a user with fewer than five interactions. Eligible sequences reserve their penultimate and final events for validation and test. Test histories include the validation item, but training targets, graph edges, and priors exclude both held-out events. Filtering and catalog indexing precede the split, so this is a transductive per-user chronological protocol, not a globally time-separated deployment simulation.

Evaluation ranks against the complete processed catalog for every eligible user. Previously observed items are masked, except that a repeated gold item remains eligible. Ties use the pessimistic rank equal to the number of candidate scores greater than or equal to the gold score. With one relevant item per user, Recall@$K$ equals Hit@$K$, and NDCG@$K$ is $\mathbf 1[r\leq K]/\log_2(r+1)$ averaged over users. We report Recall and NDCG at 5 and 10, with no evaluation-user subsampling.

For cohort analysis, short histories contain at most 10 events and long histories contain more than 10, measured on each complete filtered sequence before validation/test holdout. Membership is fixed across evaluation splits and independent of the model's context window. Each model is trained on both groups together. Short-history users account for approximately 82--85\% of evaluated users in every category.

\section{Implementation and Compared Configurations}
\label{app:implementation}
The shared collaborative item vector $g_i$ comes from a two-layer LightGCN on training interactions, averaging the initial item embedding and both propagated layers. It is combined with the cached text feature as
\begin{equation}
 e_i=\norm{\norm{W_f f_i}+\norm{g_i}},
\end{equation}
where $\norm{x}=x/\max(\lVert x\rVert_2,\epsilon)$. Validation and test interactions are excluded from graph construction and the count-based priors.

The reported runs use \texttt{state-spaces/}\allowbreak\texttt{mamba-130m-hf}, frozen 768-dimensional text features, a maximum text length of 32 tokens including the prompt, a 128-dimensional ranker, two graph layers, 32 preference prototypes, a 128-dimensional GRU, and prototype temperature 0.2. Low-rank modules use rank 16, scale 32, and dropout 0.05. Training and evaluation batch sizes are 128 and 64. The configured schedule allows 3 encoder warm-up epochs, 3 coordinator epochs, and 7 joint epochs, with learning rates $10^{-4}$, $10^{-4}$, and $5\times10^{-5}$. Single-encoder variants skip coordinator training.

Future targets use $F=3$ and $\eta=0.5$. The negative pool multiplier is 12, the target hard-negative fraction is 0.75, and its warm-up lasts two epochs per stage. Preference loss coefficients $(\lambda_p,\lambda_t,\lambda_b,\lambda_s,\lambda_h)$ are $(0.2,0.1,0.01,0.01,0.05)$; contrastive and diversity coefficients are 0.05 and 0.02. Validation NDCG@10 selects the restored best state. Joint-stage early stopping allows six unsuccessful validation checks. All included runs use seed 25252.

Table~\ref{tab:config} summarizes category-specific settings and cohort sizes. The evaluation contains 1,457 users in All Beauty, 184,584 in Baby Products, 625,563 in Sports and Outdoors, and 564,666 in Toys and Games. These are evaluated user counts rather than catalog or training-interaction counts.

We use $L$, $S$, and $P$ to denote the long-horizon item-level, short-horizon item-level, and full-history preference-transition branches in the displayed configurations. Table~\ref{tab:all_components} includes all seven nonempty combinations; every row uses the same collaborative features. The single-encoder variants are internal controls sharing the semantic and collaborative item space; they are not standalone LightGCN or reproductions of Mamba4Rec. The main model is $S+P$; $L+S+P$ adds a direct long-horizon item-level branch. Adding or removing $L$ leaves the preference input history unchanged, while single-encoder models skip coordinator training.

The component comparison and sensitivity sweeps are separate sets of recorded runs. We interpret each sweep against its own reference rows rather than combining nominally identical settings across tables, whose scores can differ. Appendix~\ref{app:runs} describes the available provenance and Appendix~\ref{app:full_catalog_groups} identifies discrepancies between overall and cohort rows. Comparisons use the displayed precision and are single-seed point estimates, without claims of statistical significance. External baseline comparisons require consistent preprocessing, item catalogs, candidate masking, and splits; the available published results do not establish such a matched benchmark.
