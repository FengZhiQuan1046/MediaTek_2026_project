\begin{abstract}
  Large-scale recommendation requires models that capture evolving user preferences while maintaining high throughput. State Space Models (SSMs) provide an efficient foundation through selective state-space modeling and recurrent inference. However, we observe that longer interaction histories do not necessarily improve recommendation quality: well-trained SSMs often rely primarily on recent interactions while benefiting little from distant history, a phenomenon we term \emph{Nearsightedness} in recommender language models. To address this, we propose RIBS (\emph{R}ecommendation via \emph{I}ntegrated \emph{B}ehavioral \emph{S}pecialists), a Mamba-based multi-agent framework that coordinates specialized recommendation roles over a shared backbone through multiple LoRA adapters. RIBS restricts the recommender agent to a short recent window, while a long-term preference agent compresses historical interactions into preference representations. A coordinator then integrates short- and long-term evidence without duplicating the language backbone. This design reduces noise from long histories while transforming distant behaviors into informative preference signals. Across diverse recommendation scenarios, RIBS consistently outperforms baselines, demonstrating the effectiveness of behavioral specialization and coordination.

\section{Introduction}
Sequential recommendation must infer what a user wants next from a history that mixes enduring interests, short-lived goals, and changing tastes. Longer histories appear to offer more evidence, yet additional interactions can also obscure the intent expressed by the latest actions. This tension matters at scale: a model must extract useful signals from long behavior sequences without making every ranking request depend on expensive language-model inference. Selective state-space models such as Mamba offer efficient sequence processing \citep{MAMBA}, but efficient processing alone does not ensure that distant interactions improve the next-item ranking.

We study this mismatch as \emph{nearsightedness} in recommender language models: a well-trained sequential ranker can draw most of its useful evidence from a few recent interactions even when a longer history is available. For example, repeated purchases of running equipment may establish an enduring interest, while recent browsing of camping products may signal a new goal. Feeding both into one sequence representation leaves the model to infer which evidence should dominate. Simply extending its input can introduce irrelevant or stale behavior; restricting it to recent events may discard a meaningful longer-term preference. The useful question is therefore how to preserve information from distant behavior without forcing every old interaction to compete directly with current intent.

We propose RIBS (\emph{R}ecommendation via \emph{I}ntegrated \emph{B}ehavioral \emph{S}pecialists), which assigns distinct roles to a short-term recommendation agent, a long-term preference agent, and a coordinator. The short-term recommendation agent observes only a short recent window to model current intent. The preference agent reads the longer interaction history and compresses it into a preference representation, including a prediction of how that preference may change. The coordinator combines their evidence for each candidate item using the predicted transition and its confidence. In this way, distant interactions inform ranking through an abstract preference signal, without giving the recommendation agent direct access to the full item-level history.

The central design choice is to separate what each agent should remember from how their evidence is combined. Recent actions remain available for responsive recommendations, while older interactions contribute through a compact view of enduring preferences. Sharing item semantics across these roles keeps the agents focused on complementary behavioral signals, and coordination lets their relative influence change with the user's context.

We evaluate the contributions of recent behavior and preference representations on four Amazon Reviews 2023 categories using full-catalog ranking. Combining the short-term and preference branches attains the highest displayed NDCG and Recall scores in all four categories of the recorded component comparison. Our contributions are the nearsightedness-motivated formulation, an architecture that coordinates a recent-history recommender with an abstract long-term preference agent over shared item semantics, and an empirical analysis of how ranking varies with the available behavioral context.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Sequential representation learning.}
SASRec uses self-attention to identify relevant items in a user's history \citep{kang2018sasrec}, while BERT4Rec instead uses bidirectional context with a Cloze objective \citep{sun2019bert4rec}. Both demonstrate how architectural and training choices influence the representation of sequential context. Our short-term recommendation agent focuses on recent item-level behavior, while the long-term preference agent summarizes a broader history through soft prototype assignments.

\paragraph{Selective state-space models.}
Mamba \cite{MAMBA} introduces input-dependent state-space dynamics for sequence modeling by combining a selective state-space recurrence with gated projections, a causal convolution, hardware-aware parallel scan. Mamba4Rec stacks Mamba layers over learned item-ID embeddings using the original selective-scan implementation \citep{liu2024mamba4rec}. Our implementation only uses a pretrained Mamba to precompute and cache item representations that remain frozen. A separate diagonal selective recurrence that retains input-dependent decay and gating of the selective SSM models user histories.

\paragraph{Semantic and collaborative item representations.}
BLaIR learns recommendation-oriented text representations by connecting item metadata with natural-language context \citep{hou2024bridging}. Because such representations derive from metadata, they are informative even for items with little interaction history. LightGCN, on the other hand, learns collaborative representations through linear neighborhood propagation on the user-item graph via aggregation \citep{he2020lightgcn}. This allows for co-consumption relationships that the previous method overlooks. We therefore combine frozen text features with trainable graph item embeddings, allowing for temporal and preference specialists to share both sources of evidence to help expand coverage on long-tail-heavy item distributions typical of product recommendations.

\paragraph{Low-rank adaptation.}
LoRA parameterizes weight updates through low-rank matrices. We borrow its parameterization for branch-specific residual transformations in the recommendation space to impose a capacity limit on updates. We train the graph embeddings, text projections, preference GRU, and its heads directly. This training stage determines which modules receive gradients as specialist, coordinator, and joint stages unfreeze different subsets.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Problem Definition}
Let $\mathcal I$ be an item catalog and $\mathcal H_{u,t}=(i_1,\ldots,i_t)$ the interactions observed for user $u$ before the next-item prediction. The task is to assign each candidate $i\in\mathcal I$ a score $z(u,i\mid\mathcal H_{u,t})$ and rank the catalog by this score. We distinguish the recent suffix $\mathcal R_{u,t}^{(W)}=(i_{\max(1,t-W+1)},\ldots,i_t)$ from the earlier prefix $(i_1,\ldots,i_{\max(0,t-W)})$. RIBS aims to model the item-level evidence in the recent suffix directly and represent longer-range evidence through a compact preference state. The coordinator combines these two sources when scoring the next item.

\subsection{Preliminary Analysis}
\label{sec:preliminary}
\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Figures/preliminary.pdf}
\caption{Behavioral evidence by distance from the next-item prediction across four Amazon Reviews 2023 categories. (a) Excess semantic cosine similarity between the next item and past items decreases rapidly with lag. (b) The change in ranking margin attributable to a past item is concentrated near the present. (c) The most recent four items account for approximately 80\% of the cumulative signal. (d) With 32 preference prototypes, excess preference-match probability can recover at longer lags in some categories. The curves are descriptive; they do not establish that removing older interactions causes an improvement.}
\label{fig:preliminary}
\end{figure*}

Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary} motivates the separation of recent item-level behavior from longer-term preference. The next item has its strongest semantic association with the most recent interactions, and this association falls quickly as lag grows (Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary}a). The estimated contribution to the next item's ranking margin is likewise concentrated in roughly the latest four positions; older positions are near zero and can be negative in the plotted categories (Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary}b). Consistently, the cumulative signal reaches approximately 80\% within the latest four items (Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary}c). These observations suggest that passing the full item-level sequence to a recency-focused recommender may introduce weak or distracting evidence.

To define the ordinate in Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary}d, let $c(i)$ be an item's assigned preference cluster and let $\mathcal C_u$ be the set of distinct clusters in user $u$'s usable history within the analyzed lag range. For the next item $y_u$, the history-based chance level is $b_u=\mathbf{1}[c(y_u)\in\mathcal C_u]/|\mathcal C_u|$: a uniform draw over the user's observed clusters, with zero chance if the target cluster is absent. For lag bin $B$, the plotted score is
\begin{equation}
 E_B=\frac{100}{|\mathcal U_B|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal U_B}
 \left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal J_{u,B}|}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal J_{u,B}}
 \mathbf{1}[c(i_{u,\ell})=c(y_u)]-b_u\right),
\end{equation}
where $i_{u,\ell}$ is the historical item at lag $\ell$, $\mathcal J_{u,B}$ contains the valid positions in bin $B$, and $\mathcal U_B$ contains users with at least one such position. Thus, matches are averaged within each user and then equally across users. The ordinate measures excess match probability in percentage points; positive values indicate agreement above the user's history-based chance level. The baseline depends on the number of distinct observed clusters, rather than being fixed at $1/32$.

Older interactions may still carry information at a coarser level. After assigning items to 32 learned preferences, the excess probability that a historical item matches the next item's preference does not uniformly decay with distance: it rises again at more distant lags in some categories (Figure~\ref{fig:preliminary}d). We therefore retain recent interactions for direct next-item modeling and summarize longer-range behavior as preference evidence for the coordinator. This is a design motivation drawn from the plotted associations, rather than a causal claim about the effect of truncating history.

\subsection{Task and Shared Item Space}
RIBS comprises a recent-behavior specialist, a preference-transition specialist, and a coordinator, operating on shared semantic--collaborative item representations. Let $\mathcal I$ be the item catalog and $\mathcal H_{u,t}=(i_1,\ldots,i_t)$ a user's observed prefix. Both specialists receive the same recent suffix of length $T=\min(t,W)$, where $W$ is the effective history-window size. We denote its item vectors by $(x_1,\ldots,x_T)$ and learn scores $z(u,i)$ for ranking the next item over $\mathcal I$. Training uses subsequent logged interactions as soft targets; validation and test each contain one target per eligible user. The sequence window limits the specialists' inputs, while collaborative representations use the training interaction graph.

For each item, we encode its text once with a frozen Mamba model. The input begins with ``Preference-aware product representation: ''. We mean-pool content-token hidden states, excluding padding and prefix tokens; if truncation leaves no content tokens, the implementation falls back to pooling non-padding tokens. Only backbone hidden states are computed; vocabulary prediction logits are unnecessary. The resulting vector $f_i$ is cached and reused during ranker training and evaluation. A trainable projection maps it to the recommendation dimension $d$.

A two-layer LightGCN \citep{he2020lightgcn} propagates user and item embeddings over training interactions. The graph representation averages the initial embeddings and the outputs of both normalized neighborhood-propagation layers. Let $g_i$ denote the resulting item vector. The shared item representation is
\begin{equation}
 e_i=\norm{\norm{W_f f_i}+\norm{g_i}},
\end{equation}
where $\norm{x}=x/\max(\lVert x\rVert_2,\epsilon)$. Validation and test interactions are excluded from graph construction and from the count-based priors described below.

\subsection{Recent-Behavior Specialist and Multi-LoRA}
The specialists and coordinator own separate low-rank residual transformations of the form
\begin{equation}
 A(x)=\frac{\alpha_{\mathrm{LoRA}}}{r}B D\,\operatorname{Dropout}(x),
\end{equation}
where $D$ maps to rank $r$ and $B$ maps back to the desired output dimension. The second matrix is zero-initialized. For an item vector $x_t$, the recent-behavior specialist updates its state as
\begin{align}
 \delta_t &= \min\{\operatorname{softplus}(b+A_\delta(x_t)),12\},\\
 a_t &= \exp(-\delta_t),\\
 h_t &= a_t\odot h_{t-1}+(1-a_t)\odot\tanh(x_t+A_v(x_t)),\\
 o_t &= \sigma(A_g(x_t))\odot h_t
       +(1-\sigma(A_g(x_t)))\odot x_t.
\end{align}
The initial state is zero, and padding leaves the state and output unchanged. The final recent-behavior representation is $s_S=\norm{o_T+A_o(o_T)}$. Its four low-rank modules parameterize the update magnitude, candidate state, output gate, and final residual. The update magnitude $\delta$ is initialized to 0.5 and learned during training.

For either specialist $b\in\{S,P\}$, candidate scores are
\begin{equation}
 z_b(u,i)=\kappa_b s_b^\top e_i,\qquad
 \kappa_b=\min\{\exp(\theta_b),20\}.
\end{equation}
The recurrence is linear in window length at fixed model dimension; full-catalog scoring additionally scales with catalog size. The low-rank transformations adapt the compact recommendation modules, while the pretrained text encoder remains frozen. The shared text projection, graph embeddings, and preference predictor are also trainable during specialist and joint optimization.

\subsection{Preference-Transition Specialist}
Let $C\in\mathbb R^{M\times d}$ contain row-normalized learned prototypes. Each interaction in the shared recent-history window is represented by a soft assignment
\begin{equation}
 q_t=\softmax(Ce_{i_t}/\tau_p).
\end{equation}
A GRU reads $(q_1,\ldots,q_T)$, and its final hidden state receives a low-rank residual and layer normalization to yield $v_T$. The current preference is the last observed assignment $q_T$. Two heads predict the next distribution and transition probability:
\begin{align}
 \widehat q_{T+1}&=\softmax(W_n v_T+b_n),\\
 \rho_T&=\sigma(w_c^\top[v_T;q_T]+b_c).
\end{align}
The predicted distribution reconstructs a preference vector,
\begin{equation}
 s_P=\norm{C^\top\widehat q_{T+1}+A_P(C^\top\widehat q_{T+1})},
\end{equation}
which scores candidates through a learned scaled inner product. Prototypes are shared among users and items within a run; separate categories train separate parameters. Prototype indices have no predefined semantic labels and need not align across runs.

\subsection{Context-Dependent Coordination}
The coordinator constructs a residual refinement of the recent-behavior state:
\begin{align}
 s_C&=\norm{s_S+A_C(s_S)},\\
 \widetilde s_C&=\norm{s_C+A_{\mathrm{score}}(s_C)},\\
 z_C(u,i)&=\kappa_C\widetilde s_C^\top e_i.
\end{align}
Here $A_C$ denotes the effective low-rank map from the recent-behavior state to the coordinator state, and $\kappa_C=\min\{\exp(\theta_C),20\}$. This refinement adds a coordinator-specific scoring path while preserving the direct specialist score.

The preference contribution depends on predicted transition probability and normalized predictive confidence:
\begin{align}
 c_T&=1-\frac{\mathcal H(\widehat q_{T+1})}{\log M},\\
 \gamma_u&=\sigma(b_\gamma+w_\gamma^\top[\rho_T;c_T]),\\
 z(u,i)&=z_C(u,i)+z_S(u,i)+\gamma_u z_P(u,i).
\end{align}
The scalar $b_\gamma$ includes the learned gate intercept, initialized so that $\gamma_u=0.2$; the context weights initially vanish. The gate subsequently learns how transition evidence and confidence should affect preference scores. The fused score is an additive combination, rather than a probability mixture over specialists. The coordinator's state refinement uses $s_S$, while preference predictions influence the final ranking through $z_P$ and $\gamma_u$.

Eight low-rank residual modules participate in this computation: four in the recent-behavior specialist, two in the preference-transition specialist, and two in the coordinator. Their parameters are distinct, while all three modules share the same item representations.

\subsection{Training Objectives}
\paragraph{Multi-step ranking.}
For each sampled prefix, we collect up to $F$ future items from the training history only. Their weights are $a_j=\eta^{j-1}/\sum_{k=1}^{F'}\eta^{k-1}$, where $F'\leq F$ is the available horizon. With candidate slate $\mathcal S$ and target position $p_j$, a ranking head minimizes
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}(z)
 =-\sum_{j=1}^{F'}a_j\log\softmax(z_{\mathcal S})_{p_j}.
\end{equation}
Bounded training prefixes are resampled each epoch. Candidate slates combine future targets with model-mined and random negatives drawn from an enlarged pool, augmented with in-batch future items. Known training positives are masked during negative scoring. The hard-negative fraction increases during warm-up. Candidate slates retain a fixed number of future-target slots: unavailable future positions repeat the last available target with zero target weight, while remaining in the softmax denominator. Sampling is over item positions, so candidate identities need not be unique.

\paragraph{Preference supervision.}
The immediate next item's prototype assignment supplies a detached target $q^+=\sg(q(i_{T+1}))$. We minimize $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pred}}=D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q^+\Vert\widehat q_{T+1})$. The transition target is
\begin{equation}
 y_{\mathrm{shift}}=\frac{\operatorname{JS}(\sg(q_T),q^+)}{\log 2},
\end{equation}
and $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{shift}}$ is binary cross-entropy between this soft target and $\rho_T$. This target measures a change in learned item assignments, rather than an annotated change in user intent.

Three additional terms control prototype learning: a KL penalty between batch-mean target assignments and the uniform distribution, a squared penalty on $CC^\top-I$, and normalized assignment entropy to encourage sharper individual assignments. Together,
\begin{align}
 \mathcal L_P={}&\lambda_p\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pred}}
 +\lambda_t\mathcal L_{\mathrm{shift}}
 +\lambda_b\mathcal L_{\mathrm{balance}}\nonumber\\
 &+\lambda_s\mathcal L_{\mathrm{separation}}
 +\lambda_h\mathcal L_{\mathrm{sharpness}}.
\end{align}
Unlike the prediction targets, the balance, separation, and sharpness terms propagate gradients through assignments or prototypes.

\paragraph{Staged optimization.}
Let $\mathcal L_S=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}(z_S)$, $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pref}}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}(z_P)$, and $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{fused}}=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}(z)$. Specialist warm-up updates the two specialists and shared item representations with
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{warm}}=\mathcal L_S+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pref}}+\mathcal L_P.
\end{equation}
Coordinator warm-up freezes these components and minimizes $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{fused}}$ with respect to the coordinator's residual maps, score scale, and preference gate. Joint training updates all these recommendation components using
\begin{align}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{joint}}={}&\frac{\mathcal L_S+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{pref}}+\mathcal L_{\mathrm{fused}}}{3}
 +\mathcal L_P\nonumber\\
 &+\lambda_{\mathrm{con}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{con}}
 +\lambda_{\mathrm{div}}\mathcal L_{\mathrm{div}}.
\end{align}
The diversity term is the batch mean of $(s_S^\top s_P)^2$, discouraging identical normalized specialist states. Contrastive alignment compares each preference state with immediate-next-item vectors in the batch:
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{con}}=-\frac{1}{B}\sum_{u=1}^{B}
 \log\frac{\sum_{v:y_v=y_u}\exp(s_{P,u}^\top e_{y_v}/\tau_c)}
 {\sum_{v=1}^{B}\exp(s_{P,u}^\top e_{y_v}/\tau_c)},
\end{equation}
where $y_u$ is the immediate next item, $B$ is batch size, and $\tau_c=0.1$. The numerator includes all batch targets sharing the same item identity. All stages optimize supervised ranking on logged training interactions; the pretrained Mamba encoder is unchanged throughout.

\subsection{Catalog Calibration}
At evaluation, training-only statistics adjust neural scores:
\begin{equation}
 \widetilde z(u,i)=z(u,i)+\alpha_p p_i+\beta_t\log(1+n_{i_T,i}),
\end{equation}
where $p_i$ is standardized log-one-plus training popularity and $n_{i_T,i}$ counts adjacent training transitions. Missing transitions contribute zero. These coefficients are configured by category. All reported results include these priors; the current experiments do not isolate their contribution from that of the neural model.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
We evaluate RIBS on four product categories to examine whether behavioral specialists provide complementary ranking evidence, how their utility varies with user-history length, and which choices of context window, prototype granularity, auxiliary supervision, and graph depth affect performance. We first compare the component configurations, then analyze the sensitivity experiments using their respective reference settings.

\subsection{Data and Evaluation Protocol}
We analyze completed Amazon Reviews 2023 runs on All Beauty, Baby Products, Sports and Outdoors, and Toys and Games \citep{hou2024bridging}. The local directory name \texttt{Full\_Beauty} refers to All Beauty, not Beauty and Personal Care. Item text concatenates available titles, subtitles, categories, features, and descriptions. Missing metadata can fall back to review text or an item identifier. The Amazon loader treats review events as implicit interactions and does not apply the rating threshold used by other dataset adapters.

Preprocessing counts raw user and item interactions and applies a minimum count of five once. It is not iterative 5-core filtering: removing low-frequency items may leave a user with fewer than five interactions. Eligible sequences reserve their penultimate and final events for validation and test. Test histories include the validation item, but training targets, graph edges, and priors exclude both held-out events. Filtering and catalog indexing precede the split, so this is a transductive per-user chronological protocol, not a globally time-separated deployment simulation.

Evaluation ranks against the complete processed catalog for every eligible user. Previously observed items are masked, except that a repeated gold item remains eligible. Ties use the pessimistic rank equal to the number of candidate scores greater than or equal to the gold score. With one relevant item per user, Recall@$K$ equals Hit@$K$, and NDCG@$K$ is $\mathbf 1[r\leq K]/\log_2(r+1)$ averaged over users. We report Recall and NDCG at 5 and 10, with no evaluation-user subsampling.

\subsection{Implementation and Compared Configurations}
The reported runs use \texttt{state-spaces/}\allowbreak\texttt{mamba-130m-hf}, frozen 768-dimensional text features, a maximum text length of 32 tokens including the prompt, a 128-dimensional ranker, two graph layers, 32 preference prototypes, a 128-dimensional GRU, and prototype temperature 0.2. Low-rank modules use rank 16, scale 32, and dropout 0.05. Training and evaluation batch sizes are 128 and 64. The configured schedule allows 3 specialist epochs, 3 coordinator epochs, and 7 joint epochs, with learning rates $10^{-4}$, $10^{-4}$, and $5\times10^{-5}$. Single-specialist variants skip coordinator training.

Future targets use $F=3$ and $\eta=0.5$. The negative pool multiplier is 12, the target hard-negative fraction is 0.75, and its warm-up lasts two epochs per stage. Preference loss coefficients $(\lambda_p,\lambda_t,\lambda_b,\lambda_s,\lambda_h)$ are $(0.2,0.1,0.01,0.01,0.05)$; contrastive and diversity coefficients are 0.05 and 0.02. Validation NDCG@10 selects the restored best state. Joint-stage early stopping allows six unsuccessful validation checks. All included runs use seed 25252.

Table~\ref{tab:config} summarizes category-specific settings and cohort sizes. The evaluation contains 1,457 users in All Beauty, 184,584 in Baby Products, 625,563 in Sports and Outdoors, and 564,666 in Toys and Games. These are evaluated user counts rather than catalog or training-interaction counts.

We use $L$, $S$, $P$, and $G$ to denote the long-horizon, short-horizon, preference-transition, and graph components. Table~\ref{tab:main} includes all seven nonempty combinations of the three specialists, with $G$ enabled throughout. The single-specialist variants are internal controls sharing the semantic and graph representation pipeline; they are not standalone LightGCN or reproductions of Mamba4Rec. The full model uses $L+S+P+G$, while $S+P+G$ tests a reduced design that retains temporal and preference evidence. Removing $L$ also shortens the preference input, and single-specialist models skip coordinator training, as described in Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\paragraph{Full model versus single specialists.}
On All Beauty, the full model achieves Recall@10 of 0.1318 and NDCG@10 of 0.1151, compared with 0.1167 and 0.1084 for the strongest single specialist, $L+G$. These correspond to relative improvements of 12.94\% and 6.18\%, respectively. On Baby Products, the corresponding NDCG@10 improvement over $L+G$ is smaller, from 0.0375 to 0.0381 (1.60\%). On Sports and Outdoors, $S+G$ is the strongest single specialist, and the full model improves its Recall@10 from 0.0412 to 0.0425 (3.16\%) and NDCG@10 from 0.0285 to 0.0297 (4.21\%). The full model also exceeds the best single specialist on both metrics at cutoff 5 in these three categories. Toys and Games is the exception: its full-model NDCG@10 of 0.0407 is slightly below the 0.0408 of $S+G$, with Recall@10 similarly decreasing from 0.0582 to 0.0579. Thus, the full architecture improves over single-specialist ranking in three categories, but the benefit is not uniform.

\paragraph{A compact specialist combination.}
The reduced $S+P+G$ model obtains the highest displayed NDCG@10 in every category: 0.1153, 0.0385, 0.0300, and 0.0409 for All Beauty, Baby Products, Sports and Outdoors, and Toys and Games, respectively. Its advantage over the full model is small in absolute terms, ranging from 0.0002 to 0.0004. It also yields the highest Recall@10 in the first three categories, whereas $S+G$ remains best on Toys and Games. On All Beauty, the full model retains a slightly higher NDCG@5, 0.1121 versus 0.1118. The results therefore favor $S+P+G$ at cutoff 10 without establishing a universally superior configuration across all metrics.

\subsection{Contribution of Behavioral Specialists}
\paragraph{Preference evidence complements temporal evidence.}
The preference-only variant is the weakest configuration on all four metrics in every category. Nevertheless, adding $P$ to $S+G$ raises NDCG@10 from 0.1079 to 0.1153 on All Beauty, from 0.0352 to 0.0385 on Baby Products, from 0.0285 to 0.0300 on Sports and Outdoors, and from 0.0408 to 0.0409 on Toys and Games. The relative increases are 6.86\%, 9.38\%, 5.26\%, and 0.25\%. Adding $P$ to $L+G$ also improves all four metrics in every category. These observations support using prototype-based preference information as complementary evidence, rather than as the only sequence representation. Because adding a specialist also changes coordination, capacity, and training objectives, these comparisons alone do not identify the mechanism responsible for the gains.

\paragraph{Longer context is not uniformly beneficial.}
The stronger temporal specialist depends on the category: $L+G$ exceeds $S+G$ on All Beauty and Baby Products, while $S+G$ performs better on Sports and Outdoors and Toys and Games. Combining $L$ and $S$ without $P$ does not exceed the strongest single specialist in NDCG@10 in any category. Conversely, adding $P$ to this two-temporal-branch configuration raises the displayed NDCG@10 in all four categories. This suggests that simply combining temporal scales is insufficient to explain the best observed rankings. However, some $L+S+G$ rows differ from the runs used for cohort analysis, so their differences should be read within Table~\ref{tab:main}. The fact that $S+P+G$ matches or exceeds the full model at cutoff 10 further indicates that a long-horizon specialist is not necessary for the strongest results in this evaluation. Its removal changes both the architecture and the preference input horizon, rather than isolating the contribution of older interactions.

\subsection{Performance across User-History Lengths}
We group users by their complete filtered sequence length before the validation/test holdout: short histories contain at most 10 events and long histories contain more than 10. Group membership is fixed across evaluation splits and independent of the model's context window. Each model is trained on both groups together. Short-history users account for approximately 82--85\% of evaluated users in every category, so aggregate metrics primarily reflect this cohort.

Table~\ref{tab:main_length_groups} reports the available cohort results. On All Beauty, full-model NDCG@10 is 0.1276 for short histories and 0.0445 for long histories. Relative to $L+G$, these are increases of 5.11\% and 27.51\%, respectively. Recall@10 also rises from 0.1292 to 0.1430 for short histories and from 0.0457 to 0.0685 for long histories. The larger relative gain for long-history users is based on only 219 users and should be interpreted accordingly. The reduced $S+P+G$ variant reaches NDCG@10 of 0.1277 and 0.0453, respectively, and matches full-model long-history Recall@10. Consequently, an improvement for users with long histories does not itself demonstrate a benefit from explicitly processing a longer sequence.

For $L+G$, short-history NDCG@10 exceeds long-history NDCG@10 in the other three categories as well: 0.0380 versus 0.0346 in Baby Products, 0.0292 versus 0.0214 in Sports and Outdoors, and 0.0418 versus 0.0306 in Toys and Games. These are descriptive differences between user groups, whose item distributions and prediction difficulty may differ. They do not show that providing more history causes worse recommendations. Full-model cohort results for these three categories are unavailable, and cohort rows drawn from different runs are not used to reconstruct the main-table totals.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the Recent-History Window}
Table~\ref{tab:short_window} varies $W\in\{2,4,6,8\}$ in $S+P+G$. Since $L$ is disabled, this changes the input window of both the short and preference specialists. Graph features still use all training interactions, so a small $W$ limits recent sequence context without removing longer-range collaborative information.

The smallest tested window, $W=2$, achieves the highest overall scores on all four metrics in Baby Products, Sports and Outdoors, and Toys and Games. As $W$ increases from 2 to 8, NDCG@10 decreases monotonically from 0.0455 to 0.0387, from 0.0416 to 0.0300, and from 0.0511 to 0.0409, respectively. Relative to $W=8$, $W=2$ improves NDCG@10 by 17.57\%, 38.67\%, and 24.94\%, and Recall@10 by 9.09\%, 25.23\%, and 15.34\%. These changes are substantially larger than the observed prototype-count and individual-loss effects. The preference for $W=2$ holds in both history-length cohorts in these three categories, including users whose full histories contain more than 10 events.

All Beauty behaves differently. Its overall NDCG@10 varies only between 0.1149 and 0.1161, with $W=8$ giving the highest value. Recall@10 is tied at 0.1338 for $W=6$ and $W=8$. Long-history NDCG@10 is instead highest at $W=2$ (0.0457), while short-history NDCG@10 favors $W=8$ (0.1286). Thus, the best observed window depends on category and cohort. These test-set sweeps characterize sensitivity; they are not a validation-selected replacement for the default setting.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Preference Granularity}
Table~\ref{tab:prefsweep} varies the number of learned prototypes, denoted by $M$ in the method and $|P|$ in the table. The full-model sweep contains settings from 2 to 128, with missing entries, while the reduced-model sweep covers $\{8,16,32,64,128\}$ in all four categories. Comparisons use the available rows within each configuration.

All Beauty favors 32 prototypes in both configurations, reaching NDCG@10 of 0.1157 for the full model and 0.1155 for $S+P+G$. More prototypes do not improve performance monotonically: increasing the count to 128 lowers these scores to 0.1142 and 0.1138. For the full model, the available NDCG@10 ranges in Sports and Outdoors, Baby Products, and Toys and Games are only 0.0296--0.0300, 0.0382--0.0386, and 0.0408--0.0411. In the reduced configuration, eight prototypes attain the best NDCG@10 in these three categories, with a tie at 16 for Toys and Games. Overall, a small prototype inventory can be competitive, while All Beauty benefits from the intermediate count of 32. The observed variations are modest, but single-seed measurements do not establish that granularity has no effect.

\subsection{Effect of Preference Auxiliary Objectives}
Table~\ref{tab:coefzero} removes each of six auxiliary terms in turn: next-preference prediction, transition detection, prototype balance, prototype separation, assignment sharpness, and contrastive alignment. The final row removes all six. These controls retain the preference branch and ranking supervision; they therefore test the auxiliary objectives rather than the presence of preference-based scoring.

No individual term is consistently necessary for the best ranking scores. Relative to the default, removing a single term changes NDCG@10 by at most 0.0002 in any category at the displayed precision. For example, removing contrastive alignment raises All Beauty NDCG@10 from 0.1155 to 0.1157, and removing balance raises Sports and Outdoors from 0.0300 to 0.0302. On Toys and Games, the default attains the highest NDCG@10 of 0.0410, but the single-term removals remain within 0.0002.

Removing all six changes NDCG@10 from 0.1155 to 0.1153 on All Beauty, 0.0300 to 0.0297 on Sports and Outdoors, 0.0384 to 0.0387 on Baby Products, and 0.0410 to 0.0409 on Toys and Games. Baby Products improves on all four ranking metrics after joint removal. Thus, the current results do not show a consistent ranking advantage from the auxiliary-loss bundle. This does not contradict the component comparison: a preference branch trained through ranking can still contribute even when its additional supervision has little observed effect. Transition-prediction quality and prototype calibration are not measured by these ranking metrics.

\subsection{Sensitivity to Graph Propagation Depth}
Table~\ref{tab:graphlayers} evaluates one to six LightGCN propagation layers in $S+P+G$, with incomplete coverage for Sports and Outdoors and Toys and Games. On All Beauty, NDCG@10 rises from 0.1133 at one layer to 0.1173 at five layers, a relative increase of 3.53\%, then decreases slightly to 0.1169 at six layers. Recall@10 reaches 0.1366 at both five and six layers. This category therefore benefits from deeper propagation among the tested settings, although the ranking metrics do not all peak at the same depth.

Baby Products favors one layer on all four metrics, with NDCG@10 of 0.0391 compared with 0.0384--0.0387 at depths two through six. Toys and Games also favors one layer among the available depths, decreasing from 0.0419 to 0.0408 and 0.0405 at two and three layers. On Sports and Outdoors, one and two layers tie at NDCG@10 of 0.0300 at the displayed precision, while three layers yield 0.0294; one layer has the highest Recall@10. The useful propagation depth is therefore category-dependent. Missing deeper runs preclude a complete depth comparison on the latter two categories, and a depth sweep without a graph-disabled control does not quantify the graph's total contribution.

\subsection{Discussion}
The experiments support a compact combination of recent temporal evidence and a preference representation: $S+P+G$ achieves the best displayed NDCG@10 across the four categories in the component comparison, while adding a long-horizon specialist yields no consistent further gain. Among the sensitivity experiments, context-window choice produces the largest observed changes on the three larger categories. Prototype count and auxiliary coefficients have smaller effects, and graph depth exhibits a different optimum across categories. This pattern suggests that the amount and source of behavioral context deserve particular attention when configuring the model. The sweeps were conducted separately, however, and do not establish the behavior of a configuration that combines their individual best settings.

The evidence supports offline ranking observations rather than a causal attribution to any one mechanism. All component rows retain graph features and catalog priors, and disabling specialists can alter both sequence horizon and optimization. Multiple-seed comparisons, matched external baselines, and controls for shared text features, graph information, priors, and coordinator behavior would be needed to resolve these contributions. The tables also contain no measurements of latency or memory, so computational savings from cached encoding remain an architectural motivation rather than a measured experimental result.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a sequential ranker that combines cached Mamba text representations, collaborative graph embeddings, temporal specialists, and explicit preference-transition prediction. The architecture keeps language encoding outside the ranking optimization loop and uses compact low-rank modules for branch-specific transformations. The recorded component comparison shows that the complete model improves over the strongest single specialist in three of four categories, while the short-plus-preference configuration attains the highest NDCG@10 in all four. Window-size experiments reveal larger changes than the prototype-count and auxiliary-loss controls, and the preferred graph depth varies across categories. These results support compact temporal--preference cooperation while showing that additional specialists and auxiliary supervision do not guarantee stronger ranking performance.

\section*{Limitations}
The evidence is limited to existing offline runs with one recorded seed per configuration. There are no confidence intervals, significance tests, or verified matched external baseline reruns. Overall full-model scores are available for all four categories, but full-model cohort scores are missing for three categories. Some overall and cohort rows correspond to different recorded runs, and the sensitivity sweeps contain missing settings. Some duplicate runs predate explicit history-mode metadata; selecting the later auditable runs improves traceability but does not establish bitwise reproducibility. The saved artifacts do not pin every run to an immutable source revision, and the suite does not persist model weights.

The ablations change more than parameter presence. Disabling the long branch truncates other sequence inputs, and single-specialist variants omit the coordinator stage. All reported variants retain graph information and category-specific ranking priors. Separate controls are needed to attribute improvements to semantic features, the preference objectives, coordination, or the priors. Although state updates scale linearly with sequence length, we have not measured end-to-end latency, peak memory, energy use, or training cost under matched hardware.

The evaluation is transductive and based on per-user chronological splits. Count filtering and catalog construction use the full observed data before splitting. Missing metadata may fall back to review content assembled before the split, potentially exposing information unavailable at recommendation time; the frequency and impact of this fallback have not been audited. Thus excluding held-out events from graph and prior construction is not a complete temporal leakage audit. The single-pass count filter also differs from iterative 5-core protocols.

Prototype transitions are self-supervised signals derived from learned item embeddings. Their identities are not human-interpretable categories, and high transition probability need not indicate a real change in personal taste. The experiments do not establish explanation faithfulness, conversational recommendation quality, cold-start generalization, or multilingual robustness. Finally, logged reviews do not describe exposure propensities or responses to alternative recommendations. Offline ranking gains do not establish causal improvements in satisfaction, retention, fairness, or user welfare.
